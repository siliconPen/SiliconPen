<h1>Chapter 2</h1><p>Before you write a single line of code or connect a sensor, you must understand the board you’re working with. The Arduino Uno may look simple, but every pin and every component on it has a purpose.</p><p>Think of this chapter as a <strong>map of your territory</strong> — once you know the landscape, building circuits becomes 10× easier.</p><hr class="section-divider"/><h1 id=\"heading-the-brain-atmega328p\"><strong>🔵 The Brain: ATmega328P</strong></h1><p>At the heart of the Arduino Uno is the <strong>ATmega328P</strong>, an 8-bit microcontroller that handles all your code and controls all pins.</p><p><img src="/images/pathway-to-arduino/understanding-the-arduino-uno-board/1.png" alt class=\"image--center mx-auto\" / /></p><p>It contains:</p><ul><li><p><strong>32 KB Flash memory</strong> → stores your program</p></li><li><p><strong>2 KB SRAM</strong> → stores variables while the program runs</p></li><li><p><strong>1 KB EEPROM</strong> → stores data permanently</p></li><li><p><strong>Clock (16 MHz crystal)</strong> → acts like the board’s heartbeat</p></li></ul><p>This tiny chip is why Arduino can read sensors, blink LEDs, run motors, and execute logic.</p><hr class="section-divider"/><h1 id=\"heading-power-section\"><strong>🔌 Power Section</strong></h1><p>The Uno can be powered in three ways:</p><ol><li><p><strong>USB (5V)</strong> – for coding and powering small circuits</p></li><li><p><strong>DC Barrel Jack (7–12V)</strong></p></li><li><p><strong>VIN Pin (7–12V)</strong></p></li></ol><p><img src="/images/pathway-to-arduino/understanding-the-arduino-uno-board/2.png" alt class=\"image--center mx-auto\" / /></p><p>And it provides these regulated outputs:</p><ul><li><p><strong>5V pin</strong> → the main power rail for most modules</p></li><li><p><strong>3.3V pin</strong> → for low-voltage sensors</p></li><li><p><strong>GND pins</strong> → always connect ground when wiring</p></li></ul><p>The power section also contains regulators that keep your board safe from voltage fluctuations. But still its a good practice to check your connections before powering the circuit!</p><hr class="section-divider"/><h1 id=\"heading-digital-pins-013\"><strong>🟩 Digital Pins (0–13)</strong></h1><p>These pins can:</p><ul><li><p>Read HIGH/LOW</p></li><li><p>Write HIGH/LOW</p></li><li><p>Control digital devices (LEDs, buzzers, relays)</p></li></ul><p><img src="/images/pathway-to-arduino/understanding-the-arduino-uno-board/3.png" alt class=\"image--center mx-auto\" / /></p><p>Special notes:</p><ul><li><p><strong>Pins 0 &amp; 1</strong> → connected to USB serial (avoid using them in projects)</p></li><li><p><strong>Pins 2 &amp; 3</strong> → support <em>external interrupts</em></p></li><li><p><strong>Pins 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11</strong> → support <strong>PWM</strong> (~analog output)</p></li></ul><p>PWM is used for:</p><ul><li><p>LED brightness control</p></li><li><p>Motor speed control</p></li><li><p>Servo timing</p></li></ul><hr class="section-divider"/><h1 id=\"heading-analog-pins-a0a5\"><strong>🟧 Analog Pins (A0–A5)</strong></h1><p>These pins read <strong>analog values</strong> using the built-in ADC (10-bit):</p><ul><li><p>Input range: <strong>0 to 1023</strong> (maps to 0–5V by default)</p></li><li><p>Perfect for sensors like temperature, LDR, potentiometer</p></li></ul><p><img src="/images/pathway-to-arduino/understanding-the-arduino-uno-board/4.png" alt class=\"image--center mx-auto\" / /></p><p>Some analog pins also support <strong>digital I/O</strong> if needed.</p><hr class="section-divider"/><h1 id=\"heading-pwm-pins-3-5-6-9-10-11\"><strong>🔄 PWM Pins (3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11)</strong></h1><p>PWM simulates an analog output using digital switching.</p><p>You’ll use PWM for:</p><ul><li><p>Fading LEDs</p></li><li><p>Controlling motors</p></li><li><p>Generating signals</p></li><li><p>Building simple DAC-like outputs</p></li></ul><p><img src="/images/pathway-to-arduino/understanding-the-arduino-uno-board/5.png" alt class=\"image--center mx-auto\" / /></p><p>You’ll explore PWM deeply later.</p><hr class="section-divider"/><h1 id=\"heading-communication-pins\"><strong>🧠 Communication Pins</strong></h1><h3 id=\"heading-1-serial-uart\"><strong>1. Serial (UART)</strong></h3><p>Pins <strong>0 (RX)</strong> and <strong>1 (TX)</strong><br />Used for USB communication and debugging.</p><h3 id=\"heading-2-i2c\"><strong>2. I2C</strong></h3><ul><li><p><strong>A4 (SDA)</strong></p></li><li><p><strong>A5 (SCL)</strong></p></li></ul><p>Used for displays, IMUs, RTC, and multi-sensor setups.</p><h3 id=\"heading-3-spi\"><strong>3. SPI</strong></h3><ul><li><p><strong>Pin 10 (SS)</strong></p></li><li><p><strong>Pin 11 (MOSI)</strong></p></li><li><p><strong>Pin 12 (MISO)</strong></p></li><li><p><strong>Pin 13 (SCK)</strong></p></li></ul><p>Used for SD cards, wireless modules, high-speed sensors.</p><hr class="section-divider"/><h1 id=\"heading-reset-button\"><strong>📍 Reset Button</strong></h1><p>Resets the microcontroller and restarts the program.</p><p><img src="/images/pathway-to-arduino/understanding-the-arduino-uno-board/6.png" alt class=\"image--center mx-auto\" / /></p><p>Useful when:</p><ul><li><p>Code gets stuck</p></li><li><p>You need to re-run initialization</p></li><li><p>Uploading a sketch after a crash</p></li></ul><hr class="section-divider"/><h1 id=\"heading-why-understanding-the-board-matters\"><strong>💡 Why Understanding the Board Matters</strong></h1><p>When you know the board’s layout:</p><ul><li><p>Wiring becomes intuitive</p></li><li><p>Errors reduce dramatically</p></li><li><p>Debugging becomes easier</p></li><li><p>Code feels more meaningful</p></li><li><p>You understand what’s <em>actually happening</em> on the hardware</p></li></ul><p>This builds the foundation for everything you will do ahead.</p>",
